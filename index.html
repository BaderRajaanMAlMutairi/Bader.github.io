Context

Origins & Background

Founded in Ismailia, Egypt in March 1928 by Hassan al-Banna, the Muslim Brotherhood emerged as a response to British colonialism and Western cultural influence — beginning as a religious reform movement before evolving into a major political force across the Arab world.

🕌
Mission

To establish an Islamic state governed by Sharia through education, social welfare, and political engagement rather than military force — operating on a principle of gradual transformation.

⚙️
Structure

A hierarchical organization led by the Supreme Guide (المرشد العام), supported by a Guidance Bureau and Shura Council — a model adopted by dozens of affiliated movements worldwide.

🌐
Reach

Active chapters and affiliated movements across the Middle East, North Africa, Europe, and beyond — one of history's most expansive transnational political networks.

Chronology

Key Moments in History

From its founding in colonial Egypt to its role in the Arab Spring, a century-long arc of repression, revival, and global influence.

1928
The Brotherhood is Founded
Hassan al-Banna establishes the organization in Ismailia with six workers from the Suez Canal Company, beginning as a youth welfare and Islamic revivalist movement.
1936–39
Rapid Expansion
The Brotherhood expands dramatically, expressing solidarity with Palestinians during the Arab Revolt. Membership grows from 800 to over 200,000 within a decade.
1949
Al-Banna Assassinated
Following government dissolution of the Brotherhood, Hassan al-Banna is assassinated in Cairo in February 1949. The organization survives and reorganizes.
1954
Nasser's Suppression
Following an assassination attempt on President Nasser, the Brotherhood is banned. Thousands imprisoned, including Sayyid Qutb, whose prison writings become globally influential.
1966
Execution of Sayyid Qutb
Sayyid Qutb is executed by hanging. His 'Milestones' becomes a foundational text for radical Islamist movements far beyond the Brotherhood itself.
2011–12
Arab Spring — Electoral Victory
Following Egypt's revolution, the Freedom and Justice Party wins parliamentary elections. Mohamed Morsi becomes Egypt's first democratically elected civilian president.
2013
Military Coup & Global Crackdown
President Morsi is removed in a military coup. Egypt, Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Russia designate the Brotherhood a terrorist organization. A wave of arrests and bans follows worldwide.
Critical Analysis

Disadvantages & Destabilizing Effects

A scholarly examination of the critiques, structural failures, and destabilizing political effects attributed to the Muslim Brotherhood across the Arab world — including its relationship to revolutionary ideology.

01
Democratic Legitimacy
Selective Embrace of Democracy

Critics argue the Brotherhood uses democratic processes instrumentally — participating in elections to gain power while maintaining internal hierarchical structures incompatible with democratic governance. Morsi's sweeping 2012 constitutional declaration embodied this tension.

02
Political Instability
Polarization of Societies

In Egypt, Tunisia, Libya, and Jordan, Brotherhood participation has often deepened societal polarization between Islamists and secularists, undermining fragile national unity.

03
Ideological Ambiguity
The Violence Question

While the Brotherhood officially renounced violence, its intellectual output — especially Sayyid Qutb's writings — provided ideological scaffolding later used by groups like al-Qaeda and ISIS.

04
Economic Governance
Governance Failures in Power

During Morsi's single year in power (2012–13), GDP growth slowed, foreign reserves fell sharply, and fuel shortages worsened. The Brotherhood lacked administrative capacity to govern effectively.

05
Regional Security
Transnational Network Concerns

Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Egypt point to the Brotherhood's transnational funding networks and cross-border political coordination as threats to regional sovereignty, alarming Gulf monarchies especially after 2011.

06
Gender & Civil Liberties
Conservative Social Agenda

The Brotherhood's social conservatism — particularly on women's rights, religious minorities, and freedom of expression — has drawn criticism from liberal movements. In power, restrictive social policies were sought through constitutional means.

⚠ Revolutionary Ideology
The Brotherhood & the Call for Revolution (ثورة)

The Brotherhood's relationship with revolutionary change is complex and contested. Officially committed to gradual, non-violent transformation, the group has nonetheless inspired and in some cases facilitated revolutionary movements. Sayyid Qutb's writings explicitly framed existing Muslim governments as illegitimate 'jahiliyya' — a theological position that later served as justification for revolutionary violence by splinter groups. During the Arab Spring, Brotherhood networks were among the most organized forces channeling popular frustration into structured political opposition. Critics in Egypt and the Gulf argue this represents a systematic strategy to destabilize secular governments by exploiting mass grievances, mobilizing street movements, and then positioning the Brotherhood as the 'safe' Islamist alternative.
